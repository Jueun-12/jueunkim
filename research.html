Reading the Reader: How Communication Shapes What Audiences See in the Same Action

Audiences form expectations about organizations before organizations speak, and those expectations shape what an organization’s words can accomplish. While prior work has largely asked how organizations raise or lower those expectations, I ask a different question: given the expectations audiences already hold, how does communication shape their interpretation of the same action? Audiences interpret an organization’s words in light of its past performance, its prior claims, and how it has been evaluated before, and organizations anticipate these interpretations when deciding what to say, how to frame it, and how much to reveal. Communication, in this sense, is not simply a matter of crafting messages; it is a process in which organizations and audiences continually read one another.

My dissertation examines this question in the setting of public firms and the analysts and investors who evaluate them, drawing on statistical analysis, experiments, and LLM-based text analysis. Essay 1, my job market paper, “Framing Strategic Change: Persuading Audiences in Offensive vs. Defensive Changes,” shows that audiences read the same strategic change through the firm’s prior performance. When a high-performing firm undertakes a change, audiences treat it as ambition; when a low-performing firm undertakes the same change, they treat it as a remedy. As a result, firms in different positions must frame identical moves differently to be seen as both necessary and feasible. Essay 2, “Two Routes to Audience Support: Expectation Alignment and Concern Resolution in Framing Strategies,” extends this analysis by addressing an unresolved tension in framing research: one stream argues that effective frames should match an audience’s construal, another that they should complement it. I reconcile these accounts by theorizing that each prescription holds under a different evaluative state, one in which audiences seek alignment with their expectations and one in which they seek resolution of their concerns. Essay 3, “The Value of Bitter Medicine: How Critical Analysts Shape Firms’ Earnings-Call Disclosures,” turns to the other side of the exchange, firms’ anticipation of their audiences. It shows that the presence of stringent analysts improves the accuracy of forecasts about a firm, not because those analysts forecast better themselves, but because anticipating a harsh evaluation leads firms to disclose more informatively, which in turn changes how much audiences can learn about the firm.

Future Research: Audience, Communication, and Organizational Standing

The figure above situates the three essays within a broader research program. It maps the primary audiences against the aspects of communication that organizations can adjust. My dissertation occupies the first column: established public firms communicating with analysts and investors, primarily through framing and disclosure. Three directions extend it; the projects below illustrate how each direction can be pursued.

The first concerns the audience. Analysts and investors are only one type of audience; the press, consumers, and activists judge organizations against different reference points, and the harder problem arises when several audiences read the same communication at once. Project A contrasts press releases and earnings calls, which address audiences with different levels of expertise, to examine how firms adapt their framing to each. Project B turns to consumers, examining how mission statements anchored in core business and financial outcomes, versus those anchored in social impact and higher-order meaning, shape consumers’ perceptions of authenticity.

The second concerns the form of communication. Organizations communicate not only through what they say but through when they speak, who speaks, and whether they speak at all. Extending the framework to these choices tests whether the same conditioning logic holds beyond framing and disclosure. Project C examines timing: whether firms release bad news when media attention is expected to be low, and whether the press learns to look at those moments. Project D turns to the messenger: who fields the hard questions on earnings calls, and whether analysts read anything into who is put forward.

The third concerns organizational standing, which the figure does not capture. My dissertation examines firms whose track records give audiences something to read. New ventures offer a contrast: audiences facing ventures without an established track record must rely on proxies, which leaves the reference point on both sides less anchored and potentially more consequential. Across all three directions, the underlying question stays the same: given what audiences already expect, how does communication change what they make of the same action?
